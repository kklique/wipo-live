# [d2-06] Discussion: The Human in the Center - Authorship and Inventorship in the Age of AI
# 발표자: Ms. Anna Tumadóttir — Chief Executive Officer, Creative Commons, Iceland  (panelist)
# Day 2 · 2026-11-10 · 예정 11:50-12:50 (Geneva) · 발언 2회 · 12단락 · 1,323단어 · 구분 방식: official

Thank you for the question, Mohammad. And thanks to my fellow panelists for their thoughtful input so far. So my name is Anna, I’m the CEO at creative commons and we are best known for our work on a suite of open licenses that allow individuals and institutions to share knowledge and creativity openly. But there is a set of standardized terms that folks can choose from in terms of how they share. And one of our common requirements here in all of our licenses is attribution because at the end of the day, the commons and sharing and creativity, it’s all about the people that build it.
And in that sense, until generative AI went mainstream a few years ago, we have been collectively building a human-centered commons. And so AI has changed that equation and put into focus some really hard challenges. I would say it’s both when you think about how you create and how you share and what the incentives are for that. But in terms of the outputs question, anytime we use an AI system, we are creating with the commons and shared global intelligence in a sense. For us, though, this is more than IP.
When we think about putting humans at the center of AI, it’s not just about who owns this copyright and AI generated outputs. It’s all about giving people meaningful opportunities to create and share knowledge and receive credit and benefit and participate in decisions about how their work and the shared knowledge is used. So it comes back to a question of participation. But we see a big need to balance this with protecting freedoms that are associated with preserving and accessing knowledge and culture because future creativity depends on that. The human in the center here needs to have access to what came before in order to learn from and build upon and ideate and create and invent.
So the central question is not really only which human contribution should qualify for IP protection but also whether the broader system is serving people and communities more broadly because a human centered approach should really think not only about creators but also about audiences and researchers and communities and these future generations. And so it’s not just a question of who receives these rights but whether these rights are going to help people participate in and benefit from creativity.

This is probably one of the single biggest challenges that is facing the commons today because we have relied on open practices to power the commons for so long. But they were based on recognition and they were based on certain conditions being in place. One of our earliest executive directors who now sits on our board, he characterized the early days of sharing on the web as being this battle between all rights reserved and the rights respected. And I think some of my fellow panelists have already highlighted this dynamic is really present again. But no creator or innovator is working in isolation.
Like I said earlier, we build on a shared foundation of creativity and knowledge and culture. And when we think about how we are going to protect new creative works, we really do have to preserve this ability of others to access and learn from and build upon their own knowledge and their own expertise. And the they build upon them. And I just want to highlight that because there is a user rights side to this equation. But at the same time, AI is also challenging the sustainability of people and communities and institutions and infrastructure that are making this shared knowledge and culture available.
Especially those who are trying to do so broadly for access and reuse. And those are the ecosystems that we need to find a way to support them. This is where Creative Commons talks a lot about reciprocity. We need to encourage reciprocity in these systems because otherwise we will not be able to fuel continued creativity. If AI systems are going to benefit so greatly from our shared collective knowledge, then they should contribute to the thriving Creative Commons that has brought us this far rather than depleting them.
And since this is a panel hosted by I do want to say that while copyright is an important part of the answer, it alone will not provide all of the answers to support human creativity in the age of AI. And in much the same way, while licensing is maybe part of the solution, it won’t be the whole solution or it can’t be the whole solution because there is so much that we stand to lose if we end up with an information ecosystem where all access is contingent on the existence of a license license. Can you imagine if the open web that we have all grown up with and benefited from for the last two or three decades just becomes a temporary experiment in human history and then we go back to, you know, needing a lot of privilege and funding to be able to access things like education or just basic information? So I think we really need to take a step back and think about some of the values and the goals that are at stake here more holistically and in particular in terms of what creators need because they need recognition and to some degree, moral rights offer this important right of attribution but that’s not implemented in all jurisdictions. Creators need agency and that includes meaningful choices about how work is shared and reused but it should also respect copyright limitations and exceptions in the public’s rights.
Creators need opportunities to earn a living and copyright protection doesn’t necessarily translate into meaningful remuneration for individual creators. So strengthening copyright alone will not resolve the economic challenges that creators face and have faced for a long time. Creators also need access to knowledge and culture that will expire new work. So we should similarly assume that expanding these exclusive rights will not really address these needs. But finally, I think it’s a question of needing the opportunity to participate.
And so this includes people and communities who have historically faced barriers either due to, you know, equity concerns or resource concerns but they have been inhibited from full cultural and technological participation. So I want to go back to just the overarching theme of the panel here, this idea of putting humans at the center because it also raises the question of which humans? You know, there’s different communities and cultures and they have different expectations about either individual credit or collective knowledge and appropriate reuse structures. So supporting cultural diversity means we need opportunities for communities to shape the use of their contributions. And again, still safeguarding the freedoms that allow others, especially smaller developers and researchers to continue to participate.
So I guess I would say in closing what we have heard from other panelists, we are treating AI as a tool and there’s a really complex legal environment that it’s operating in. So these questions of rights around inputs to these systems, they vary by jurisdiction. We have heard a little bit about the EU and the U.S. These questions of rights related to outputs are still largely undecided because I would say it’s hard if not impossible to take something that is as complicated as the human creative process and turn it into a mathematical equation to decide where rights should sit. How much did I do? How much did the machine do?
How much did I build on the work of others and so forth? So there’s a pretty long road ahead of us here. And I think I would just encourage all of us to keep thinking about how we strike this balance between those who contribute their efforts and those who then benefit from that reuse. Yes. Yes.
