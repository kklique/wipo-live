# [d2-06] Discussion: The Human in the Center - Authorship and Inventorship in the Age of AI
# 발표자: Ms. Claudia Catelin — Director of Policy, The Publishers Association, United Kingdom  (panelist)
# Day 2 · 2026-11-10 · 예정 11:50-12:50 (Geneva) · 발언 2회 · 7단락 · 747단어 · 구분 방식: official

My name is Claudia Catlin and I’m from the London based publishers association. I think that putting the human at the center means three things for our sector. Firstly, AI is a tool. It’s complementary to human creativity but it cannot replace it. So publishers are using AI in a variety of different ways whether that be using it to detect for potential research integrity issues in academic publishing, using it to provide personalized learning experiences in education publishing or indeed to achieve operational efficiencies or to automate day-to-day tasks.
That being said, there needs to be human oversight across all of these uses of AI and that’s what our members champion and we firmly believe that AI supports not replaces human authorship. The second meaning of sort of putting the human at the center is ensuring that creators can retain their rights. So the right to remuneration, the right to permission and the right to control. AI companies have engaged in mass copyright theft, diminishing creator remuneration and control and this problem is existential for publishers and our sector because it effectively reduces the incentive for them to continue to create high quality works on which reliable and trusted AI models depend. Thirdly, keeping the human at the center means giving people and readers the authentic human creatorship that they want to see, in our case read.
Does it matter if a book is written using AI? The evidence suggests yes. There was a recent study conducted by our equivalent in Denmark which sampled 12,500 people and it found that 73% of people do not want to read AI generated books. Only 3% do. There have been similar figures in a study in Sweden and I think all of this shows that people want to continue to read and consume high quality human authored works and that we need to protect creativity.
In practical terms, what does all of this mean for policymakers? I would say two things. The first is that there is a need to promote and retain robust copyright frameworks and in doing so avoiding introducing any over broad copyright exceptions. Secondly, introducing transparency mechanisms which essentially enable AI developers to disclose details of the works used in their training for rights holders to see. And I think that in order to yield high quality outputs it is necessary that creators continue to create high quality trusted inputs.
So I think those things can help to achieve that.

I want to start by saying that publishers embrace AI in their operations and they perform a crucial role in underpinning AI development both as the curators of high quality content that AI developers wish to use to train their models and also as sort of innovators in their own right and I think to safeguard human creativity, the rights of authors, trusted content as well, it’s essential that AI models are developed in a legal and sustainable and ethical way which speaks to sort of responsible AI use if you like and the way to achieve that is through licensing as you intimated. This year we published a report which was I think really the first comprehensive survey of publishers and how they are licensing their content for AI use. The findings were very insightful. I think they found that the licensing market for AI amongst publishers is established and thriving and growing with the number of publishers active in the market set to double this year and it will include all major academic publishers as well.
Secondly, the report proves that publishers high quality content is increasingly valued by AI firms and that there are legal means for accessing this content if AI developers wish to do so. And I’d just like to make a sort of broader point that I think too often this sort of AI copyright debate hits the tech companies on one side and the creative industries on the other but that need not be the case because I think we industries have shared objectives in terms of yielding high quality trusted outputs and that’s in the interests of sort of businesses, government and society as a whole in terms of deploying AI to the masses and the best way to do that is through licensing frameworks which are established and growing. Well, this is very interesting and what you have just published I think will be very useful and I hope it can be spread around the globe.
