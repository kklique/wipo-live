Our ministry is moving towards AI based examination systems using specialized AI models developed in-house. We have already introduced AI into patent searches, trademark classification and design image searches to improve examination quality and we will continue to expand its use to other areas.
To make IP information more accessible to the public, we also plan to introduce an AI powered assistant that explains complex patent documents in plain language. The second is responding to the growing number of AI assisted IP applications. AI has significantly lowered the barriers to filing IP applications. In the first half of 2026, patent filings by individuals in Korea increased by 130% compared to last year. However, as filing becomes easier, we are also seeing a rise in low quality or fraudulent IP applications involving the misuse of AI referred to as IP slop.
To address this problem, our ministry has introduced an early screening system that detects such applications and sends out alerts. We also plan to review our fee system for individual applicants to discourage excessive filings resulting from low application costs. In addition, we will pursue legal and institutional reforms to address the misuse of AI during the application process such as the manipulation of experimental data. Director General Daren Tang, Excellencies, Ladies and Gentlemen, I’m sorry I can’t join you in person. Unfortunately, for all its advances, AI still cannot put me in two different places at the same time. Thankfully, technology allows me to deliver this prerecorded speech and I’m delighted to share some thoughts with you. First, I thank WIPO for convening this important inaugural global forum. AI is fundamentally transforming how we create and innovate, challenging our very basic assumptions underlying our IP systems.
I think we cannot nor should we stand in the way of progress. As policymakers, our task is to ensure that AI and IP reinforce rather than undermine each other so that innovation and human creativity can thrive together. I would like to suggest three areas where we can work together. First, sustaining human creativity alongside AI innovation. AI models learn from vast quantities of human created content, whilst increasingly generating content themselves.
How do we encourage AI innovation without weakening incentives for human creativity? Even before generative AI became mainstream, our copyright act in Singapore already permitted computational data analysis including AI training using lawfully accessed works subject to safeguards. Our approach is to regulate with a light touch, particularly when technology is evolving so rapidly. This we believe encourages innovation. We also believe in giving creators and innovators the space to experiment, develop new ideas and bring them to market without being constrained by overly prescriptive rules.
At the same time, they must have the confidence that their works are protected. That’s the environment we are seeking to provide in Singapore, one that encourages innovation underpinned by a strong, predictable IP framework. But getting this balance right is an ongoing process. That’s why we recently launched a public consultation on AI and IP. We are seeking views on strengthening our legal framework, including how copyright should apply to AI training and outputs, how best to recognize human creativity in AI assisted works and how our patent framework should respond to AI assisted inventions.
I believe these are challenges all of us face and also all of us can tackle them together. For example, we can explore practical approaches to licensing and rights management that facilitate lawful AI development, protect creators’ rights and value human creativity. Second, making different national approaches work together. Countries will understandably adopt different approaches, but fragmented rules could create legal uncertainty and opportunities for regulatory arbitrage. As a small, open economy, Singapore places particular importance on a predictable, interoperable international IP system.
We need not agree on everything, but we can find common ground and make our different approaches work better together. Singapore is keen to contribute our experience. Through WIPO, we can explore common principles, develop guidance on different national approaches and find practical solutions to cross-border challenges. Third, ensuring AI expands opportunities for everyone. AI could give individuals and small businesses capabilities once reserved for larger organizations.
Yet, concentrated control over data, computing power and IP could limit these opportunities. We must, therefore, guard against anticompetitive practices that exclude smaller players, whilst helping them acquire the knowledge and expertise to harness AI and manage their IP effectively. Singapore has long supported international capacity building in IP. This April, for example, we partnered WIPO to organize an executive program on emerging AI and IP challenges, bringing together professionals from nine countries. As Singapore assumes the ASEAN Chairmanship next year, we look forward to building on such initiatives to help more businesses and creators harness AI.
Our longstanding partnerships with WIPO and IP offices around the world reflect our belief that we achieve more by learning from one another and then combining our strengths. IP was designed to serve human ingenuity. We must ensure that it can continue to do so even in the age of AI. I thank all of you for the opportunity to speak, and I wish you fruitful discussions. I hope to join you in person at the next forum.
Thank you.

Mr. Director General, Excellencies, Distinguished Delegates, ladies and gentlemen, at the outset I wish to extend my highest commendations to the World Intellectual Property Organization and to you, Mr. Director General, for your visionary leadership in convening this inaugural global forum on Intellectual Property and Artificial Intelligence. By transitioning from exploratory dialogues to this structured, inclusive and multilateral platform, WIPO has fostered a collaborative space where all nations actively participate in shaping the global governance of Artificial Intelligence and Intellectual Property. In doing so, this forum provides a vital opportunity to move beyond theory, allowing us to exchange concrete best practices, harmonize regulatory insights and collaborative architect a jurisprudence that is balanced, equitable globally and globally aligned.
Earlier this year, Zimbabwe proudly launched its national Artificial Intelligence strategy for 2026 to 2030. We do not view AI merely as a novel technology but as a catalyst for inclusive growth, enhanced public service delivery and economic diversification. While the state will fulfill its duty to provide robust regulatory direction and enabling legal environment, the true Vanguard of this revolution will be the unyielding creativity of our people, nurtured within our universities and propelled by our private sector. Zimbabwe’s vision is rooted in fostering local agency and authorship within this digital economy, a principle that serves as the bedrock of our national Artificial Intelligence strategy. This strategy does not merely seek to adopt technology.
It intentionally builds domestic capacity across critical pillars from sustainable digital infrastructure to targeted human capital development. Our young innovators are already translating this policy into practice, pioneering localized solutions to uniquely domestic challenges. A prime example is crop fix, an AI-enabled pest and disease diagnostic tool developed by a youth-led startup. To sustain this momentum, we are aggressively investing in human capital through our new software developer skills development hub and specialized trainings for agricultural extension offices. However, for this ecosystem to thrive, our legal frameworks must keep pace.
Our current copyright and patent laws were not designed for the complexities of the digital age, making IP reform our most immediate priority. Mr. DG, the primary legislation governing Intellectual Property rights for creators, performers and broadcasts organization in Zimbabwe is the copyright and neighboring rights act, chapter 26.05. The act defines an author strictly as an individual. Consequently, works generated entirely by AI face a legal vacuum. Therefore, the law must urgently clarify ownership rights and define the threshold of human input required for legal protection.
Furthermore, Mr. DG, AI training models rely on the consumption of vast datasets. Our regulatory framework remains silent on whether fair dealing exemptions cover commercial AI training. Our creators require absolute assurance of credit and compensation just as our developers need clear, predictable rules to innovate with confidence. Our legislation also defines an inventor as the natural person who devised the invention, leaving AI unrecognized. While global consensus largely favors human inventorship, the landscape remains fragmented.
We must determine how to assign ownership when researchers, AI developers and data owners all contribute to a single breakthrough. Additionally, the black box nature of AI complicates patent disclosure requirements and our laws must carefully delineate between an unpatentable algorithm and a patentable technical solution such as an AI system that improves crop yields or diagnosis of diseases. Crucially, Zimbabwe stresses the urgent need to protect traditional knowledge, cultural expressions and African data. AI systems consume vast datasets. We must, therefore, enforce mechanisms for consent, recognition and equitable benefit sharing, ensuring that local communities are never marginalized.
Mr. Director General, Zimbabwe does not face these questions in isolation. If nations fragment into divergent regulatory frameworks, cross-border innovation will stall under the weight of legal uncertainty. International cooperation has always been the cornerstone of IP consistency and the dawn of AI makes collaboration more critical than ever. Our collective mission is to architect an Intellectual Property system that is innovative yet balanced, technologically responsive yet profoundly human centric and protective of rights while supportive of public access. We must ensure that AI serves to bridge rather than widen the digital divide, empowering developing nations to create, evaluate and participate fully in the global knowledge economy.
Zimbabwe looks forward to collaborating closely with WIPO, fellow Member States and all global stakeholders to ensure our international Intellectual Property system remains resilient, equitable and fit for the edge of artificial intelligence. I thank you.
