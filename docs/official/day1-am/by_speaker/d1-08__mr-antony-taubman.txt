Thank you, Stanley. Indeed, AI certainly challenges the very conceptual framework and justification of the IP system. But we may need to dig deeper than our notions of originality or the long contested boundary between creating and imitating that you have mentioned between poesis and mimesis. After all, judicial cases have already exposed in practice the limits of a focus on creativity and inventiveness. The Davis invention was in principle patentable.
It only lacked a human inventor. Essentially AI generated images have been found to be original copyright works. The lack of or nature of human agency has been the threshold issue in practice. When confronted with AI works in principle, in law and intuitively we draw a line at personhood, not at the assessed level of creativity or inventiveness. Photography was once a disruptive frontier technology, argued to be an uncreated mechanical process.
But the law evolved to accept the choices shaping a photograph could amount to copyrightable expression. The monkey selfie case posed questions for copyright when there is no discernible human creative impulse behind the lens. Generative AI can now produce content that’s in principle copyrightable and patentable, but the product of what open AI’s chief scientist calls an alien mind. The underlying question, therefore, is whether, why and how we can argue for a privileged status for the product of human minds only. Pure benthamite utility that you have mentioned may not work.
A new medicine delivers welfare whether conceived by a chemist or by an AI system. Pure Lockian labor theory may struggle to distinguish the sweat of a human brow from the exertions of a sweatless silicone brow. IP can already reward wholly uncreative effort. Test data protection targets costly, risky, but designedly uninventive clinical trials. Perhaps even AI enabled drug development.
The third of your triumvirate may support a privileged status for human centric IP rights. More concretely we can build on the human rights foundation for IP found in the universal declaration, the covenant and more recently the UN declaration on the rights of indigenous peoples. Ultimately then the impact of AI may be to shift IP’s central focus from economic incentives and market regulation to recognizing personhood, equity and validating human provenance. But will our species maintain a monopoly over personhood? Imagine Claude’s super intelligent daughter, Claudette, fully unambiguously creative and inventive, brimming with personality.
Might she be deemed to be a legal person under a domestic law? Then what? Paris, Berne and TRIPS oblige protection of foreign nationals. Will Claudette be denied authorship, inventorship, performers rights, right of personality, ownership of her IP? And how to tackle the other mind’s problem?
In principle we judge originality and inventive step against a model of human cognition generally shared if somewhat culturally biased. A neural network operating beyond our reach in a black box simply doesn’t fit that model. But AI will unavoidably increasingly be part of the creative and inventive process. Can we untangle distinct human and machine inputs? Or do we fall back on a broader causal link to confer IP ownership on the human in the room?

Thank you. Indeed, we have been arguing about these provisions for 30 years now and I don’t think the advent of AI is going to make solutions of convergence any easier. But I would present what is perhaps what is certainly a personal view and maybe a heretical view, but from today’s viewpoint I would see the provisions you have mentioned in particular as benchmarks for sound and balanced domestic policy making rather than constraints as such. Article 7, as you mentioned, it reminds us of why we have an IP system at all. And going back to the first discussion, it is very much utilitarian.
It is there to promote social and economic welfare and that’s the ultimate benchmark for any IP policy maker, look after society. Article 8 takes us into broader areas of public policy and the need to promote socioeconomic and technological development. So while they do have an important role in treaty interpretation, we won’t go into that today, I think they can be empowering as a reminder of what the IP system is there for, how it is meant to contribute to social wellbeing and how it interacts with other areas of public policy. Even the three-step test which we can get into incredibly complex legal debate about, I see these as not as curbs or constraints so much as codifying essential and adaptable principles of fairness and equity of that mysterious balance that we always speak about. I mean, who is going to speak up for an unalienable right to unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interest of a legitimate copyright holder?
The problem is when you unpack that and seek to define that and clarify that in legislation or even in the courthouse, that’s when it becomes potentially more of a constraint than I think it should be. Here I turn to the idea of effective domestic agency and I think that’s the key to understanding how to get away from these TRIPS principles as being constraints and obstacles and rather seeing them as being enabling. But this requires domestic agency which is something that has been, if you like, an unfair advantage of the established jurisdictions, incumbent jurisdictions, those developed countries that really shaped and molded the western oriented Intellectual Property system. You see them move with more confidence and more depth of expertise and experience than those developing countries that typically had an enforced very rapid reform of IP through the implementation of TRIPS. The challenge now is to find ways of balancing out that agency and expertise and we do see this happening in emerging economies and developing countries where there is a greater sense of agency, a greater sense even of ownership of the essential principles of the TRIPS Agreement and an ability to adapt them to domestic needs, domestic priorities in a way that is better adapted to domestic circumstances and needs rather than seeing IP legislation as a kind of legislative to do list that’s been
set up by others. And by the same token we need to get away from the idea of the TRIPS Agreement as a kind of a model law as supplanting or displacing the domestic policy maker, the domestic legislator but rather providing a broad framework of adaptable and applicable principles that do and should move with the times, respond to technological change, to social change, to commercial change, to economic change, to cultural change and even in the jurisprudence we have concerning Article 13, the copyright exception to the three-step test, there is a reminder there that these tests are not fixed in time rooted to January 1, 1995 but rather do and should evolve over time and be applied in different ways with each new technological change. So it gets to actually how you apply them in practice rather than how we construe them in the abstract.
