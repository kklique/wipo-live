# [d1-03] Ministerial-level Remarks
# 발표자: H.E. Mr. Ziyambi Ziyambi — Minister of Justice, Legal and Parliamentary Affairs, Zimbabwe  (speaker)
# Day 1 · 2026-11-09 · 예정 10:10-10:45 (Geneva) · 발언 1회 · 8단락 · 736단어 · 구분 방식: official

I wish you a productive and inspiring forum. Thank you. Mr. Director General, Excellencies, Distinguished Delegates, ladies and gentlemen, at the outset I wish to extend my highest commendations to the World Intellectual Property Organization and to you, Mr. Director General, for your visionary leadership in convening this inaugural global forum on Intellectual Property and Artificial Intelligence. By transitioning from exploratory dialogues to this structured, inclusive and multilateral platform, WIPO has fostered a collaborative space where all nations actively participate in shaping the global governance of Artificial Intelligence and Intellectual Property. In doing so, this forum provides a vital opportunity to move beyond theory, allowing us to exchange concrete best practices, harmonize regulatory insights and collaborative architect a jurisprudence that is balanced, equitable globally and globally aligned.
Earlier this year, Zimbabwe proudly launched its national Artificial Intelligence strategy for 2026 to 2030. We do not view AI merely as a novel technology but as a catalyst for inclusive growth, enhanced public service delivery and economic diversification. While the state will fulfill its duty to provide robust regulatory direction and enabling legal environment, the true Vanguard of this revolution will be the unyielding creativity of our people, nurtured within our universities and propelled by our private sector. Zimbabwe’s vision is rooted in fostering local agency and authorship within this digital economy, a principle that serves as the bedrock of our national Artificial Intelligence strategy. This strategy does not merely seek to adopt technology.
It intentionally builds domestic capacity across critical pillars from sustainable digital infrastructure to targeted human capital development. Our young innovators are already translating this policy into practice, pioneering localized solutions to uniquely domestic challenges. A prime example is crop fix, an AI-enabled pest and disease diagnostic tool developed by a youth-led startup. To sustain this momentum, we are aggressively investing in human capital through our new software developer skills development hub and specialized trainings for agricultural extension offices. However, for this ecosystem to thrive, our legal frameworks must keep pace.
Our current copyright and patent laws were not designed for the complexities of the digital age, making IP reform our most immediate priority. Mr. DG, the primary legislation governing Intellectual Property rights for creators, performers and broadcasts organization in Zimbabwe is the copyright and neighboring rights act, chapter 26.05. The act defines an author strictly as an individual. Consequently, works generated entirely by AI face a legal vacuum. Therefore, the law must urgently clarify ownership rights and define the threshold of human input required for legal protection.
Furthermore, Mr. DG, AI training models rely on the consumption of vast datasets. Our regulatory framework remains silent on whether fair dealing exemptions cover commercial AI training. Our creators require absolute assurance of credit and compensation just as our developers need clear, predictable rules to innovate with confidence. Our legislation also defines an inventor as the natural person who devised the invention, leaving AI unrecognized. While global consensus largely favors human inventorship, the landscape remains fragmented.
We must determine how to assign ownership when researchers, AI developers and data owners all contribute to a single breakthrough. Additionally, the black box nature of AI complicates patent disclosure requirements and our laws must carefully delineate between an unpatentable algorithm and a patentable technical solution such as an AI system that improves crop yields or diagnosis of diseases. Crucially, Zimbabwe stresses the urgent need to protect traditional knowledge, cultural expressions and African data. AI systems consume vast datasets. We must, therefore, enforce mechanisms for consent, recognition and equitable benefit sharing, ensuring that local communities are never marginalized.
Mr. Director General, Zimbabwe does not face these questions in isolation. If nations fragment into divergent regulatory frameworks, cross-border innovation will stall under the weight of legal uncertainty. International cooperation has always been the cornerstone of IP consistency and the dawn of AI makes collaboration more critical than ever. Our collective mission is to architect an Intellectual Property system that is innovative yet balanced, technologically responsive yet profoundly human centric and protective of rights while supportive of public access. We must ensure that AI serves to bridge rather than widen the digital divide, empowering developing nations to create, evaluate and participate fully in the global knowledge economy.
Zimbabwe looks forward to collaborating closely with WIPO, fellow Member States and all global stakeholders to ensure our international Intellectual Property system remains resilient, equitable and fit for the edge of artificial intelligence. I thank you.
