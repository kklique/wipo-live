# [d1-13] One-Stop Shop: Keeping up with IP and AI
# 발표자: Mr. Daryl Lim — H. Laddie Montague Jr. Chair in Law and Associate Dean for Research and Strategic Partnerships, Penn State Dickinson Law, United States of America  (panelist)
# Day 1 · 2026-11-09 · 예정 15:10-16:10 (Geneva) · 발언 1회 · 18단락 · 1,594단어 · 구분 방식: official

Good afternoon, everybody. Good morning, good evening. Please imagine with me a small newspaper whose readers usually find its reporting through Google search. The publisher wants those links to remain visible on Google but she may object to her reporting being used in AI generated answers that give readers less reason to visit the newspaper itself. What if she cannot refuse the second use without jeopardizing the first?
Now consider an independent AI developer building a service for, let’s say, Wolof, a language spoken widely in Senegal. Research has documented how poorly leading language models perform on Wolof and other underrepresented African languages. The developer wants to obtain the permissions she needs but she cannot afford to negotiate with hundreds of publishers and other rights holders. One wants to restrict access. The other wants to obtain it.
Yet they face a related difficulty because copyright choices are shaped not only by what the law permits but the systems through which people must exercise those choices. So I want to invite us in a few minutes, we have to explore three questions together. First, can right holders meaningfully refuse a use? Second, can developers negotiate permission at a workable cost? And third, once permission is granted, can anyone actually reliably establish what it covers?
So first, take the publisher and think of two switches. First, she wants to have such indexing on and second, a particular AI use off. If the two switches were wired together, refusing the AI use may mean losing the readers that she depends on. And on June 3rd, the U.K. competition and market authority imposed an interesting conduct requirement on Google concerning publishers and AI features in search following its designation with strategic market status in the general search services market. And it provides for more effective publisher controls over specified AI users together with attribution and other safeguards.
But what makes the intervention by the CMA interesting is the institutional choice. A competition authority acted to protect the practical ability of publishers and IP owner to make decisions about the users of their content. Recognizing that a right to refuse is one thing, but making it possible to actually refuse without sacrificing an unrelated commercial opportunity is quite another. Second now, let’s talk about competitive entry and return to the developer who wants permission. Alina talked about this in 2024, the news core and open AI announced an agreement covering current and archived journalism.
Such arrangements can offer important benefits. Publishers obtain compensation. Developers obtain consent on agreed terms. But the relevant cost has got two components. One is the payment for using copyrighted works and the other is the expense of actually finding the rights holders establishing who can grant which rights and negotiating the agreements.
So consider the wall of developer. You have a major tech company on one hand who may have a team of a lot of people, a great team capable of managing hundreds of agreements. The wall of developer, a small, maybe medium-sized company, probably does not. And that’s why design of licensing actually matters as much as the headline price. Here India and Europe have proposed quite different approaches to solving this issue.
An Indian Government Committee proposed a framework called one nation, one license, one payment. So the idea here is the AI developers gain legal rights to use lawfully accessed copyright protected works for training without needing individual negotiations, no opt-out rights from creators. Developers cannot legally bypass paywalls or technical protection measures and must pay a flat rate percentage based on their gross global revenue. Now, that could reduce transactions costs. But rights holders would lose the ability to withhold those rights from training and difficult questions remain about what the rates would be and how to properly distribute those, the revenue.
In Europe, the European Parliament voted recently by 460 to 71 in March to adopt a nonbinding resolution encouraging voluntary sector specific collective licensing. It also opposed a single global flat rate license for training and called for respect to rights holders who refuse training users. And it also suggested a role for the EU IP office, the EIPO in machine readable reservation of rights and voluntary sector specific licensing. These approaches, they reflect different choices about how licensing should work. India’s proposal would replace individual negotiations with a mandatory system of regeneration.
Europe’s proposal seeks to reduce transactions costs while preserving the right to refuse. The point is that the way that licensing is organized affects not only what developers pay but also how much control rights holders retain and who has the resource to participate. And that brings me to an interesting tension in the United States. In September, the Justice Department warned that requiring licenses for AI training could favor the largest tech companies which can afford the costs while excluding smaller competitors. And the Justice Department is one of the two agencies federally in charge of any trust policy.
Interestingly, the White House itself recommended in March that Congress consider allowing rights holders to negotiate collectively with AI developers without incurring any trust liability. So even within the U.S. administration’s approach, there is a distinction between whether permission is legally required and how permission could be organized. And politics may change again. After last week’s elections, the Democrats will control both houses of Congress by January and three senior democratic lawmakers have challenged the Justice Department’s reasoning in the litigation, particularly its appeal to national security when courts decide fair use. And you heard about fair use when Ed spoke about it earlier.
Now, they also emphasize that the Justice Department overstepped its authority, pointing out that the filing reportedly surprised the government’s own copyright and patent experts, the USPTO and the Copyright Office. Now, what can we expect? Oversight may increase. A presidential veto remains possible. And courts will continue to determine fair use.
But I would not expect a settled U.S. position soon. And here there is one caution for IP policymakers, which is we should not assume that copyright is the only or even decisive barrier confronting small or medium-sized AI developers. If access to computing and distribution remains concentrated, making training material cheaper may produce substantial savings for incumbent firms without much doing much to help to broaden participation. So before changing the copyright rules in the name of competition, it is important for us to ask whether the benefits will actually reach the people that we hope they will reach or mainly benefit the companies already best placed to take advantage of them. I have spoken about meaningful choice.
I have spoken about competitive entry. Let me end by talking about practical rights. Now, WIPO’s Copyright Committee is due to meet later this month with a new scoping study on AI training and copyright. Member States can and probably will disagree about exceptions, permissions, remuneration. But there are practical questions we can investigate without agreeing on one worldwide copyright rule.
Suppose a developer acquires a dataset marked as license for commercial use. That sounds reassuring. But what if the dataset provider was authorized to collect the underlying works but not to redistribute them for AI training? The developer may have a license from the dataset provider without having the permissions needed for the underlying material. And that’s why here we need to examine the entire dataset life cycle, how works are sourced, redistributed, retained and incorporated into subsequent datasets.
Because a legal license does not by itself establish that earlier copying was authorized and the challenge is therefore not simply to record permissions but to establish where they came from, what they cover, whether they actually remain valid as the works that are reused. And this is where WIPO’s AI infrastructure interchange could make a real contribution. It can bring right holders, developers and technical specialists together to test what rights information systems can actually do. And here I suggest three tests for those systems. First, can the system distinguish indexing, training and retrieval?
Second, can it carry permissions and their limitations through subsequent dataset users? And third, can a small or medium-sized rights holder or developer use it without prohibitive cost? So in closing, let me return to the publisher and the Walloff AI developer. The publisher needs to be able to say no and that no must be respected without an unjustified loss of access to readers. The AI developer needs an affordable way to establish what she may lawfully use and enough evidence to be able to rely on the permission she actually receives.
Now, competition authorities, they have an important role in addressing certain constraints imposed by powerful intermediaries. Courts and legislatures, they can determine the legal scope of rights and exceptions, but it is the IP offices and WIPO, those of you in the room or those of you listening today who can help people communicate and verify the permissions on which those precise choices depend. So my invitation to Member States is to test these systems against real cases, including small and medium publishers, independent AI developers and languages that the leading language models, LLM models neglect. Find out where the permissions breakdown, where the costs accumulate and where a refusal actually ceases to be meaningful because the question is as we consider these broad issues, not only who has the legal power to say no, it is whether the systems that surround copyright allow that answer to be heard and allow a genuine yes to be trusted. Thank you very much.
