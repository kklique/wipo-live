Of course, the main question remains how do publishers, can publishers signal their right reservations, their opt-outs, their licensing preferences in a machine readable way to bots?
So to go from no crawl to an actual deal, the toolkit that I have decided to present to you has five building blocks. There are opt-out signals, so just know you cannot crawl. There are licensing protocols that ask for a deal in order to crawl. There are marketplaces where publishers actually get paid, collective licenses which pull licensing, pulling rights, and publisher coalitions. The opt-out signals just tell bots to stop.
The EU-born standard is TDM rep which adds opt-out licensing URL to server or HTML. It is free. It is open. It applies to all content. IPTC instead is photo metadata.
It embeds opt-out inside the image files. It is free and open, but it applies only to images and photos and, of course, metadata can be stripped. C2PA is instead a digital passport for content. Its main focus is to say how a content was created and then edited, and the opt-out is just one of many signals that it carries. It is very complex.
It applies to all content. It is the only one which is supported by big tech. Then there are licensing protocols where the publisher asks for a deal. COMP, for example, works this way. The bot arrives.
COMP asks for identification and whether it has a permission to be there. If the bot does not have a license, COMP directs the bot to where it can get a license and then it is still there at the door to control whether they got the license and therefore they can access or not. The RFP standard, real simple licensing, adds licensing terms to robot TXT. While robot TXT right now only says yes or no, with RSL standards it is possible for the publisher to indicate instead other things like, of course, yes, no or also you can crawl or you can only you can crawl if you pay this amount or you pay for inference or for use it is easy, it is free and open and it applies to all content.
