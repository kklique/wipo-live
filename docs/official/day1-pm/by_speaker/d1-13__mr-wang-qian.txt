# [d1-13] One-Stop Shop: Keeping up with IP and AI
# 발표자: Mr. Wang Qian — Professor of Law, East China University of Political Science and Law, China  (panelist)
# Day 1 · 2026-11-09 · 예정 15:10-16:10 (Geneva) · 발언 1회 · 12단락 · 1,363단어 · 구분 방식: official

Good afternoon, everybody. It is my great pleasure to present the key developments in 2026 concerning AI and IP policies, legislation and cases in China and other Asia countries. As AI technology is blowing up so fast it is always necessary to strike a proper balance between two competing interests. The first challenge is to promote AI technology while protecting the interests of IP holders. China is a major player in AI and Chinese tech companies are pushing hard to classify using works to train AI as fair use but others strongly oppose to sacrificing copyright as the cost of AI development.
Those two intense and conflicting demands have led to some uncertainty and ambiguity in relevant policies in China. In March of this year the state council which is the central government of China issued the outline of the five-year plan which set goals for the country’s development over the next five years. The outline calls for accelerating the building of AI and establishing a system for the fair use of the training data. Does this mean that using large number of copyrighted works to train AI qualifies as fair use? Some government agencies seem to think so but the data explicitly mentioned here is energy, transportation, manufacturing, education, health and financial data.
No mention of literary or artistic works. So you could also read it as the government not taking a position on using works to train AI. It seems that the government is keeping policies flexible and open to interpretation. On September 7 this year the Supreme People’s Court issued the judicial opinions on AI related cases. This judicial document gives courts specific guidance on how to apply the law in cases involving AI and the lower courts must follow it.
The draft version of this document had the provision about the training AI and fair use but it was removed from the final version. At the press conference releasing the document the Supreme Court explained and I’m quoting here during the drafting and internal discussion there were significant disagreements regarding the issue of using works to train AI without permission which still requires further study. So no provisions have been made on this matter for the time being. It seems that we still don’t have a plan in place that offers solutions or a clear path forward. The challenge is still there to overcome.
In 2026 some other Asia countries have responded to this issue in their own ways. Korea released its guide which takes a case by case approach to assessing whether the use of copyrighted works in AI training constitutes fair use with particular emphasis on whether such use is transformative. Kazakhstan’s new IP law which came into effect this year adopts a unique approach to using works for AI training. It excludes such use from the scope of exclusive rights including the right to reproduction. So it has nothing to do with the traditional copyright exceptions and limitations.
The new law also allows works to be used to train AI as long as copyright holders have not made a machine readable reservation. Saudi Arabia has amended its copyright law to introduce a copyright exception permitting the use of copyrighted works for AI training which applies only where the works have been lawfully published and their copies have been lawfully obtained. Compared with this relatively permissive legislative approaches to AI training some countries have adopted more restrictive laws. Vietnam amended its law to introduce a TPM exception allowing non-commercial use of copyrighted works for AI training provided that the data is lawfully accessed. For certain types of TPM that meet the three-step test right holders can’t opt out through machine readable reservations.
For commercial AI training however permission from the copyright owner is required. India has proposed a statutory licensing scheme for using works to train AI so copyright owners can get paid. However in the case of ANI media and open AI during the stage of a preliminary injunction application the court ruled the open AI storage of copyrighted works for AI training was a fair dealing and did not constitute copyright infringement. The divergent policies and legislation adopted by Asian countries reflect their distinct policy objectives and the industry realities even though all countries agree that the three-step test is the bottom line the existence of the diverse solutions may continue for a long time. The second challenge is to promote AI services while preventing their misuse.
These days almost everybody has AI app on their phone and AI services are booming but sometimes AI produces content that is substantially similar to copyrighted works. Should AI developers and service providers be held liable for copyright infringement? The opinions issued by the Supreme Court this year we just mentioned request the courts to make case by case judgments. Courts must take all the relevant factors into consideration like whether the training data came from lawful sources, whether AI developers and service providers took the necessary steps to prevent AI from generating infringing content and whether they made profits from it. The opinions also let AI developers off the hook if they can prove that their algorithm was reasonably designed and the infringing output was technically avoidable or just a one-time fluke or was deliberately induced by users.
What stands out in the Supreme Court’s latest opinions is that it does not provide as many people expected a notice and take down procedure for the AI developers’ liability. It follows that there is no safe harbor for the liability of AI developers and it does not impose product liability either, meaning no strict liability applies. This shows that China has not adopted AI exceptionalism regarding the liability of AI developers and service providers. The third challenge is about encouraging the use of AI as a tool for innovation while avoiding the extensive expansion of IP protection. In this regard, the Chinese courts have handled the largest number of cases about AIGC and judicial policy is becoming increasingly clear.
As you may recall, back in November 2023, the Beijing Internet Court became the first court worldwide to recognize that an image generated by AI constitutes an artistic work protected by copyright law. Four other Chinese courts subsequently issued the similar rulings. However, over the past two years, courts have clearly shifted their stance. More than a dozen courts have now rejected the idea that such images qualify for copyright protection in similar cases. The Guangzhou Internet court recently disclosed that in 2025 alone, more than 600 cases have been filed alleging copyright infringement of AI generated images but none of them were supported by the court.
The chief judge made it clear that stockpiling large numbers of AI generated images and monetizing them through litigation constitute abuse of copyright protection. On the other hand, two courts in China have ruled this year that short dramas produced with AI technology are copyrightable. The reasoning is that these dramas are not produced in a single step from text prompts. Creators have to write scripts and then make original selections and arrangements of numerous 10 to 15 AI generated video clips so the final product reflects human intellectual creativity. Similarly, the new copyright decree makes it clear that content only gets copyright protection when humans have made a significant and decisive contribution to ensure that the final result accurately reflects their creative intent rather than the random or automatic arrangement of the algorithm.
The draft registration guidelines released by the Philippines emphasize that works are eligible for registration only when they contain original expressions resulting from human authors’ creative contribution and meet the originality requirements merely providing text prompts or instructions to AI should not by itself establish sufficient human creative contribution. Obviously, there is growing consensus in Asia that protecting content autonomously produced by AI will put the fundamental values in the principles of copyright law in crosshairs so content entirely generated by AI cannot be protected by copyright but AI can still serve as a meaningful tool for human authors to create works.
