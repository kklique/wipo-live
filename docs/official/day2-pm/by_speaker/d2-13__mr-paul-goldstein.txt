These are great, the three examples given and Terry’s overarching point are really great pointers to how public policy should raise and address these issues. Three related points. First, it is understandable, particularly within an organization that has Intellectual Property as its middle name, to think of solutions in terms of what kind of property rights are we going to give to these new technologies, these new inventions. In fact, the social welfare norm is not to give property rights at all to information, to let information spread free. That’s the starting point, not what kind of Intellectual Property rights do we give.
One of the founders of the American Republic, Thomas Jefferson, used a wonderful metaphor when somebody wrote to him about the desirability of having a patent system in the United States. He said we really should not have property rights over information. Someone who lights his candle from my candle increases the light available to him without diminishing the light available to me. So the basic principle, the starting point is Intellectual Property are islands of monopoly within a general sea of competition. That’s number one.
Number two, the kind of Intellectual Property you choose and the only rationale for Intellectual Property is that there will be no investment in the manufacture of candles unless you give property rights to it. That being the rationale, the kind of Intellectual Property you choose to induce innovation will turn on the kind of innovation you want. This goes back 35 years ago when people were certain industry incumbents were lobbying for copyright protection for computer programs. It ultimately resulted in it becoming the international norm of IP protection. One couldn’t imagine a less well conceived Intellectual Property for inducing investment in innovation than copyright.
The reason Terry gave earlier is that copyright doesn’t protect ideas. It only protects the expression of those ideas. So that’s number two. Ask what it is you want in terms of innovation and then craft and do you need it to induce innovation and then craft the Intellectual Property around it. The third and final point is to pay more attention than we have particularly in the United States but worldwide to the competition law in the U.S. We call it antitrust, the competition law aspects of all of this.
The incumbents back in 1991 that were seeking to use copyright to interfere with developments in interoperability of computer programs should have been laboring under competition law scrutiny. I think there are comparable developments to be watched today in incumbent AI companies using Intellectual Property and licenses and contracts to build walls around themselves and for themselves to become entrenched incumbents and that really deserves continued scrutiny from a competition law perspective.
