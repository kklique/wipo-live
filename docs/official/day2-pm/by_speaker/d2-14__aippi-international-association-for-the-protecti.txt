So Excellencies, Distinguished Delegates, speakers, panelists, colleagues and friends, ladies and gentlemen, as we bring this inaugural global forum on IP and AI to a close, let me bring by thanking all our Member States, speakers, moderators and participants for making these two days such a rich, stimulating and forward-looking exchange. I would like to thank our capable Chair, Ambassador Toribayev for his excellent Chairpersonship and I’m delighted to share that the participation numbers has been extraordinary. On day one alone yesterday we were joined by over 2500 participants in the room or on Zoom and on the webcast. This is phenomenal.
That is a remarkable response to a first-time event and a clear indication of the relevance of our discussions. We discovered that humans can still manage to break some of the systems, AI systems, and we had to restart our systems more than a few times. But we also reached many more people in many more languages than would otherwise have been possible. So this was an achievement. This is what innovation within the UN system can look like, not simply taking about new technologies but testing them, learning from them, and using them to serve people better. At the opening, the Director General invited us to reflect on some fundamental questions about the future of Intellectual Property in the age of AI and the place of human beings in an increasingly AI driven world.
AI is expanding the boundaries of what we can achieve, but it is also challenging some of our assumptions about creativity, innovation, ownership, and responsibility. Over those two days, we have explored those questions from many different perspectives. We also time-traveled back to WIPO’s first AI and IP symposium in 1991, reminding us something important. I was already born by that time. The IP system has faced major technological transformations before.
Copyright has evolved through photography, radio, television, film, the Internet and streaming, and it will need to evolve again. But the speed and the space of AI means that we cannot simply assume that the approaches of the past will always be sufficient. Whatever the outcome of ongoing legal cases around the world, I would like to return to a point made by the Director General at the opening. As a global community, we should seek common ground and principles that can guide us as AI continues to develop. That does not mean we need to agree on every rule or that every country must take the same approach.
Our legal systems, economic circumstances, and policy priorities will differ, which is reality. But we should be able to build shared understanding around some fundamental questions. How do we ensure that creators and innovators continue to be recognized and rewarded? How do we support the development and responsible use of AI? How do we make sure that the opportunities of this technology are accessible to countries, businesses, and communities around the world everywhere?
