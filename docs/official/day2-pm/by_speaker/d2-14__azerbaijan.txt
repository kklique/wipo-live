It is dedicated to the interaction between artificial intelligence and Intellectual Property. As we are well aware, Artificial Intelligence is irreversible. Its influence today to a greater or lesser extent extends to the majority of areas of our lives. However, from the perspective of Intellectual Property, at the center of this transformation are the fields of creativity and law. Artificial Intelligence is one of the greatest technological breakthroughs.
It is changing the face of civilization. And together with big data analytics, it simultaneously poses serious challenges. The main feature of autonomous Artificial Intelligence is the ability to produce results without direct human creative involvement. For example, in its specific case, Generative Artificial Intelligence learns from huge datasets and creates texts, music, images and videos which if created by a human would typically be protected by copyright. Currently, a human centered approach is legally and doctrinally accepted meaning that only a human can be the author or rights holder and within this approach there are indeed different opinions.
The spectrum of views ranges from the assumption that such content falls into the public domain which is fully consistent with current legislation. That is, free use of the works. However, it is still subject to criticism as it acts as an obstacle to the development of new technologies. So how can the activities of Artificial Intelligence be regulated by law? The solution to this problem naturally can be approached from two perspectives.
One is the Delegate, meaning the existing legislation and the other is the Delegate, meaning the delegated authority, that is, desirable or necessary legislation. Let’s start with the Delegate. The current legislation is in effect. It does not recognize Artificial Intelligence as a legal subject and it transfers, you could say, the rights to the physical persons who are related to the object. Legal protection has been established autonomously.
However, the results remain problematic because recognizing any user request which is called a prompt as an act of creativity leads to the devaluation of the concept of creative work. An equally important question is the legality of using protected works. This is for training Artificial Intelligence without the consent of the copyright holder. There are differing opinions regarding the regime of legal protection. For example, protections similar to that of derivative works or photographic works or works that have no authors, such as the transmission by an organization of broadcast or cable television, noncreative databases, sound recordings, know-how or by analogy, including the topologies of integrated microchips as well as the recognition of objects as works made for hire and objects protected under a special sui generis regime, another fundamentally different option is the recognition of authorship of
a work created by Artificial Intelligence and the existence of rights for the subject of that activity. This can and should be implemented only within the framework of desirable legislation. Unlike the anthropocentric approach, this approach is electron centric. Obviously, it contradicts existing legislation. However, even if we were to accept this hypothetical possibility, we will see that such a rights holder can only have the right of authorship and the right to name.
The other rights simply make no sense. And they cannot have property rights. The essence of these rights is either material benefit or legal liability for actions. In other words, even in the hypothetical case mentioned, legal capacity will be limited. There is also an approach based on hybrid authorship, that is, when both Artificial Intelligence and a human are recognized as coauthors.
However, its implementation will require adding an additional clause to the legislation providing for the possibility of such coauthorship. In this case, the legal capacity of Artificial Intelligence will still be limited. And such a work must be excluded from the list of copyright objects. And this approach is applied in the United States of America. Now, a few words about algorithmization and associative thinking because this is closely related to the concept of the legal capacity of Artificial Intelligence.
Algorithmization implies automated data processing and the search for solutions based on computational algorithms. Whereas human thinking relies on associative connections, intuition, memory and personal experience. Associative connections form associative memory. In humans, unlike humans, Artificial Intelligence does not have consciousness. It does not experience emotions.
And it has no personal experience or memories that belong to a human being. As a result, statistics replaces intuition because the machine is looking for mathematical patterns not meaning ultimately modern fuzzy neural networks operate on the basis of mathematics and statistics. Relying on these, Artificial Intelligence does not think but rather simulates thinking. The human brain is not oriented towards computational enumeration but towards associative comparison or comparison with an imperfectly defined standard. This is a fundamental difference.
It is of particular importance when evaluating the creative activity of Artificial Intelligence. It also relates to determining its possible legal subjectivity. What are the main difficulties in determining legal subjectivity? The problem encompasses three interrelated aspects. The first is philosophical.
It involves rethinking the criteria for legal subjectivity, namely consciousness, reason and social necessity as well as possible recognition of a third category Artificial Intelligence. This is the distinction between a thing and a legal person. It involves legally reconciling authorship with rights, duties and liability. This includes the possibility of recognizing electronic entities. And in terms of praxology, the distribution of rights, risk insurance, licensing and mechanisms of responsibility, what changes are possible within the framework of desirable legislation that is in the event of a Lego forendum?
Over time, of course, a stage is possible where applications will be accepted and in cases where they are not submitted under the authorship of Artificial Intelligence with the distribution of rights among creators, owners and users. In our opinion, the mechanism for protecting copyright is very convenient by analogy with the protection of noncreative assets and the establishment of rights for electronic entities, the use of a neighboring rights mechanism is also possible. If initially Artificial Intelligence is not recognized as a full-fledged electronic entity, then in the distant future, as its participation in the economy becomes more active, it is quite possible that there will be a review and Artificial Intelligence will be granted. More comprehensive rights as a subject. The use of protection is also quite possible with the introduction of graduated levels of legal subjectivity of Artificial Intelligence practically starting from zero when Artificial Intelligence exists simply as an object, up to limited rights for specific legal actions.
What does all this depend on? It all depends on philosophical and ontological discussions and on a rethinking of what makes us human beings as subjects of law. Biology, consciousness, reason or social necessity. And also on how Artificial Intelligence technologies themselves will manifest. What decisions related to Artificial Intelligence are desirable given the existing legislation?
I emphasize given the existing legislation. First of all, it seems advisable to develop a special international protocol similar to the Rome Convention on related rights which would regulate the rights and status of content created by Artificial Intelligence, separate from traditional copyrighted works. Secondly, it seems desirable to allow the use of protected materials for training Artificial Intelligence for commercial and research purposes provided that fair compensation is maintained by analogy with private copying such as making a copy. Thirdly, it seems advisable to establish a special protection regime for digital works created autonomously. This includes a limited term of protection from 25 to 30 years.
In addition to this, there is another possible solution. It is the creation of a system of mandatory licensing. This would involve directing a certain portion of payments towards the use of content created by Artificial Intelligence. Funds to support human creativity. So, generative Artificial Intelligence, autonomous Artificial Intelligence and Artificial Intelligence in general, they have not created the problem of inefficiency in Intellectual Property and specifically copyright.
However, they have significantly exacerbated it, leading to a crisis situation. The further development of the legal system must be based not only on preserving existing principles but also on creating new legal mechanisms and if necessary, the necessity and new models of legal subjectivity. In all of this, in a world where Artificial Intelligence is bringing about a revolution, technology is a tool that requires human control. Thank you for your attention. Thank you very much.
