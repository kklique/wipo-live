Mr. Chairman, ladies and gentlemen, all Arab countries wish to express their appreciation for the World Intellectual Property Organization for organizing this first forum. It provides a platform for dialogue led by Member States and highlights its importance. At this particular time, artificial intelligence technologies are advancing at a pace that exceeds the ability of legal frameworks to keep up. The ultimate measure of its success remains the extent to which it serves humanity and fosters creativity.
In this context, the League of Arab States would like to highlight a fundamental issue for us.

Rundi is understood by 98% of Burundians and it is the only language spoken and understood by more than 80% of the population. However, it is present on less than 1% of buy websites. This is not insignificant. More than 80% of the training data for these models is in a single language, English. And it is estimated that only 5% of the more than 7,000 living languages recorded have a sufficient digital footprint to be represented in AI in the long run.
A language absent from the Web is absent from AI and with it, the knowledge it carries. An AI that only knows Africa or Asia through the eyes of others cannot reflect the real world. We talk a lot about open models but an open model that doesn’t understand my language remains to me a closed model. For a Bambara-speaking entrepreneur in a small town in Mali or an artist who expresses themselves in Fulani in Lome, if they cannot work in their own language, openness remains a promise on paper. Protection of rights and data diversity reinforce each other and it is in this spirit that the Francophonie recently brought together in Alexandria decision makers, AI entrepreneurs, data specialists and librarians.
And their conclusions will, of course, be shared with the Secretariat of PIME. We invite them to participate in the follow-up. We call for three directions. First, to measure. We need tools and methods to measure the presence of languages and cultures in training corpora and thus open the black boxes they represent.
No AI should be called inclusive unless we measure its language proficiency, the language proficiency of those it claims to serve. Second, encourage alternative solutions, smaller, more targeted, transparent models based on locally produced data. This requires supporting digitization while respecting the rights of the content from spaces that are currently underrepresented. Third, finally, to cooperate between the OMP, UNESCO and the linguistic spaces. The Francophonie is, of course, ready to contribute actively to this.

Thank you, Chair. I speak on behalf of Asia Pacific broadcasting union, the world’s largest broadcasting union. I would like to add an important observation from the broadcasting sector. When we discuss AI and IP, there is sometimes a tendency to consider individual questions separately, which is the status of training material, the protection of a model, the use of AI in creating content and the rights in the resulting output. For broadcasters, these questions can arise within a single production and across several jurisdictions.
That makes legal clarity particularly important. It is not only a question of determining whether a particular use is permissible. Organizations, especially public service broadcasters also need to understand how rights and responsibilities move through the different stages of the AI enabled production and distribution process. This is especially relevant in the Asia Pacific region where broadcasters operate across very different legal, technological and economic environment. I therefore think an important part of the continuing IP discussions is to look at these issues across the value chain rather than treating each question as an isolated legal problem.

Now I would like to call Mr. Roy Meltzer, Patent Attorney AI, AIPPI. You have the floor. Thank you, Chair. I speak on behalf of AIPPI which brings together IP practitioners from around the world. The Director General opened the forum with Venice, IP as the institution that moves value from the mind of the inventor into the economy.
For centuries practitioners did exactly that. We took an idea and we turned it into value through the IP rights. Today alongside with that patent practitioners take on a new responsibility. The idea often reach us already processed by AI and AI is very generous. Asks to describe one invention and you get 40 fluffy pages, 12 embodiments and the short history of the wheel.
Somewhere inside usually around page 27 hides the actual innovation and it is our role to extract it. The inventor is human and it is the human inventor we represent. Our new responsibility is extraction and crystallization, finding the inventor own contribution inside all of the noise, separating it from the machine speculation and make sure that the real that it is real and enabled. Ideally we do it before the AI enters the picture so that as asked human judgment is recorded and not lost. This responsibility brings three challenges.
First, transparency. Clients need to know how AI is used on their confidential matters and offices and courts will increasingly ask where the human contribution is. Practitioners are best placed to document it. If we rebuild and we have to build this habit now. Second, accountability.
An AI model does not sign a filing. We do. As Mr. Ito put it, no one can stand up and say the AI thought it was a good idea. As we learned from Korea, they told us that patent filing by individual rose by 130% in the first half of this year alongside with a rise of low quality AI generated applications. Practitioners must be the one who used as quality filters, not as amplifiers.
And third, training. Singapore told us that new examiners are asked not to use AI for four months and admitted that this does not really happen. We face the same problem. The task on which young attorneys learned to craft the first draft, the first office actions to response now takes seconds. We must teach judgment deliberately.
Otherwise as the filmmaker on the authorship panel feared we will get practitioner who draft like AI with no vision and no skill. The IP offices told us it all comes down to people. Practitioners are the people on the other side of the examiner desks. AIPPI stands ready to bring this experience into the dialogue. Thank you.
