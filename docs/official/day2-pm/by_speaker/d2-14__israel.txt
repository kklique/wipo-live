The discussions over the past two days have offered a truly multidisciplinary and multijurisdictional lens on how legal frameworks and market participants are adapting to emerging business models within a fragmented legal landscape. In navigating the shifts, WIPO’s committees and bodies play an indispensable role in facilitating structured dialogue. For example, the ongoing work within the SCP maps out key patent law issues and enables Member States to prioritize topics and examine these complex questions holistically. Similarly, in the field of copyright, the SCCR carries out fundamental work by fostering dialogue and expert exchanges. Alongside such bodies, forms such as this one are vital as they allow us to address overarching cross-cutting issues that do not fit neatly into existing frameworks.
Earlier today, we touched upon the challenges of integrating IP considerations into the broader AI landscape. As a dynamic, outward-looking innovation hub, Israel monitors international developments closely while refraining from sweeping horizontal legislation. Instead, the government’s approach focuses on sector-specific intervention only where a distinct market failure or regulatory barrier is identified, deliberately favoring agile tools over rigid rules to preserve technological and economic flexibility. A tangible illustration of this approach is a forthcoming legal opinion by the Attorney General addressing the public sector’s adoption of artificial intelligence. An example of AI adoption in the public sector is the Israeli Patent Office’s strategic vision which focuses on integrating AI across the patent examination process to improve efficiency, safeguard quality, and ensure consistency as presented by the head of the ILPO.
The Attorney General’s legal opinion focuses on constitutional and administrative considerations. As part of the work, the Ministry of Justice’s IP division is formulating dedicated guidelines on copyright, providing government bodies with practical guidance on the responsible use of generative AI tools and their outputs. This opinion applies well-established legal doctrines to emerging technologies, including a creative analogy drawn from the orphan works doctrine adapted for instances where underlying training datasets cannot be verified. The proposed operational framework introduces a multi-step risk mitigation process which includes inter alia prioritizing systems trained on licensed content or public domain materials, utilizing enterprise services for government use, conducting similarly checks to the extent technically feasible, and establishing a swift notice and take down procedure for third-party claims. While formally targeted as the public sector, this opinion holds significant potential to inform private sector practices.
Recognizing this broad relevance, the drafting process was accompanied by extensive multi-stakeholder engagement, including an interministerial working group of government legal advisors focused on public sector AI deployment, consultations with leading academic scholars and a dedicated round table with representatives from the Israeli Bar Association.
