Good morning, everyone. My name is Mateo, and I’m the head of the third-party relationship at Elsevia, a scientific publishing company which is part of the Relax Group. I’m very pleased to present this work developed with colleagues. I was not alone. I would especially like to acknowledge Pascal from Wiley who deserves much of the credit for all this work.
Together we mapped the scholarly publishing workflow and where AI intersected with it. The aim was to show how industry work, where AI meets their workflow as a starting point for discussing the infrastructure, challenges and opportunities involved. The exercise was valuable to us because it helped us to think through how we look at the topic. By putting the activities together, we could see how closely they depend on one another and how something that happens at one stage can affect what happens much later. The starting point of the workflow is especially clear in scientific publishing and scholarly publishing is not a straight line.
The published record supports discovery. Discovery informs new research and that research creates new work. So we represented the workflow as a cycle. I’ll use the map to explain how that cycle works, where AI intersects with it and what the exercise helps us to understand. So let’s follow the cycle clockwise starting at the top.
Yes. Researchers prepare and carry out their work, write it up and submit it to a journal. The journal assesses the submission. If it proceeds, expert editor and reviewer help evaluate it. Accepted work then goes to production and is published.
The published facilitate this process and subject matter expertise is central, especially in editorial decision and peer review. AI can support tasks such as initial screening, but I will not describe the whole process as machine working while a person simply check it. In many important decisions, the human drives and AI support it. Once published, work needs to be visible. Other researchers need to find it, read it and build on it.
They may also analyze it computationally or use scientific AI tool slower. That work can then inform new research, bringing us back to the beginning. The circle is simplified by the capture of the basic idea. Science builds on science. Scale is one reason the process needs both system and expertise.
As one example, Elsevier reported 4.2 million submissions to its 3,000 journal in 2025 with about 800,000 articles published that single year. AI now appears across nearly every stage of this life cycle. It can help people work with information at greater speed and scale, for example, by supporting screening, analysis and discovery. There are real potential benefits. AI can help researchers and editors handle demanding tasks and identify patterns in large collection of information.
In areas such as medical imaging, for example, it can help flag possible pattern for specialists to examine. But support is not the same as independent judgment. AI tool can miss context or produce information that sounds convincing but is inaccurate or out of date. In science and especially medicine, that matter a lot because people may rely on information to make important decision. So the question is not only what AI can do but also what information it relies on and whether the context around that information remains available.
That brings me to the infrastructure which has been put at the center of the map. Scholarly publishing has relied on shared infrastructure for many years. At the center of the map are four kinds of infrastructure that support the life cycle, identifiers and metadata, access and entitlement, rights and licensing and integrity and provenance. Scholarly publishing has been the system over many years. Together they help identify works and author, describe access and permitted use, show where content came from and record changes such as correction or attraction.
AI system may use scholarly content beyond established publishing workflows. They may not always receive or retain all of those signals. For example, a journal may correct or retract the paper after an AI supported system has already used an earlier version. If the update doesn’t reach that system, it may continue to surface the earlier information. The issue is whether correction travel with the content and whether people can trace which version a system relied on.
That connection between established infrastructure and AI system is one of the things the map helps us to see. The correction example brings us to the loops in the center of the map. They show connection that may not yet be working consistently across the system. The red dashed lines mark pathway the group identified as contested. They highlight places where question around rights and use remain open.
There are six gaps on the map. I will focus here on 2. G2 which concerns how correction and retraction reach indexes and AI system that may have used an earlier version which we have just seen in the example before. And G3 raises the question of how AI related use can be audited and linked to remuneration where relevant. The circles show how knowledge moves.
These two gaps show where important connection may need attention. For me, one of the most valuable parts of this work was looking at AI through a system-wide lens. It moved our discussion beyond individual tool and helped us see how the stages depend on one another. The exercise also made clear that scholarly publishing already has substantial infrastructure and expertise. The challenge is ensuring the relevant information travel as content moves through the cycle and into AI system.
I want to acknowledge the value of WIPO creating space for this broad cross-industry view. It helped us better understand the system and gave us a clearer way to discuss where technical collaboration and perhaps other action may be needed.
