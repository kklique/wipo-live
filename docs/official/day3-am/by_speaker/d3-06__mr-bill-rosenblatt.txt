Hello. Thanks, Garrett. Yes. So my answer to that segues pretty well out of what Andy just said which is that the closer you are to the real world supply chain, the better things tend to work. And so, for example, in the music industry, there is general agreement about the atomic units of commerce in music which is musical works and sound recordings and we have identifiers for those that are pretty well understood in the supply chain, especially compared to their analogs in many other creative sectors where they are less well understood.
And where these kinds of identifiers have failed, it is precisely because of a supply chain inadequacy. For example, music services not getting ISWCs along with music assets from record labels because the record labels basically opted not to be involved with that when the industry transitioned to streaming. And another good example that I would point to is ONIX in the book publishing industry which is a metadata standard for books in the supply chain meaning initially print books and that’s exceeded because it was necessary to get online book selling to work efficiently so that online book sellers could get metadata in one format instead of thousands of different formats. And the same is true in the music supply chain with a couple of the DDEX standards. When you have supply chain issues, you have common incentives for everyone to get things right.
And I’ll note finally that on this diagram, the supply chain issues tend to relate to the first four of those bullet points and the industry has generally treated the rest of the bullet points as nice to haves rather than must haves.

So I would look at it slightly differently and say the metadata doesn’t necessarily have to travel with the asset. It just has to be accessible somewhere, somehow and this has to do with provenance information, knowing who the rights holders are, knowing what the works involved are. And in many cases we found ways to work around the lack of that information being available. One example is in music, we have a cottage industry of companies that help rights holders get licenses or recover royalties for the use of samples and beats after the fact. And so they use fingerprinting technology, ACR, other signals to do things like that.
That’s fairly well known, fairly well established and we all understand that to be at least partially doable if complicated and imperfect. So you can think of the AI problem as that on steroids essentially. It’s a geometric increase in complexity and the essential problem is it’s not at all clear how much of that information can be recovered after the fact. So there are companies that are trying to do that and we’ll see how far they get. But I would like to offer a contrast example in educational publishing where there is another cottage industry which is rights management systems for tracking all the permissionable or royaltable assets that go into a typical textbook which can be in the thousands.
And because you have a B2B market there essentially you are not selling to consumers per se. You are selling to institutions or whatnot. There is more of a need for accountability there. And so those systems are better generally about storing all that information in a place where it can be accessed.

Well, I will say two things even though there is limited time here. First, the people who tend to create metadata standards are often not the ones who have to use them. And there are some examples from that I have experienced where a metadata standard was created with hundreds or thousands of fields and then the people who actually had to populate those fields wanted there to be three or four or whatever. It just, you know, you have to be realistic. The other point that I want to make is there was a concept that I was exposed to around 10 years ago in something called the open music initiative called minimum viable interoperability, MVI.
I didn’t create this but I really like the concept and I have used it in various things that I have done where you look at a bunch of interoperable or potentially interoperable systems, distinct systems such as metadata standards across different creative industries and you think about what is the minimum viable way that we can interoperate among them based on use cases and you go with that instead of trying to create yet another ultimate metadata standard to rule them all and I will end with I think it was a saying by a Dutch computer scientist, hopefully someone will correct me if I’m wrong, who said something like this, paraphrasing, the wonderful thing about standards is that there are so many of them to choose from.

So I have two things. One thing that I think this AAA and this Working Group can help with is it’s really hard in this very fast moving environment to get out in front of ad hoc solutions that occur out of convenience among limited sets of parties. But there’s a real need to do that and I think this initiative can help with that. And then the other thing I want to mention is something that maybe this group can’t help with but WIPO perhaps could which is that we talk about a lot of different technologies that have to do with metadata and rights management and so on, but we don’t have good ways of evaluating how good they are. The tests or evaluations are often locked up behind NDAs or whatnot.
Everyone is afraid of publicizing anything. We need better mechanisms to ensure that everyone is made aware of these technologies and how effective or ineffective they are.
