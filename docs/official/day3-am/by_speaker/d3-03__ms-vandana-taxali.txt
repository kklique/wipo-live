Artworks are created, exhibited, sold, collected and licensed often repeatedly and each of these stages generates those records. But those records are often fragmented. So meaning they’re decentralized, they’re stored in different locations and it’s not always easy to piece them together. But now let’s look at each stage of the art life cycle and in the end we’ll look at what happens to these records when AI enters the picture.
So the first is conception and creation. So an artist will first look at creating the work and so an artist will document how work was made, the materials used and any third party content. And these records can later establish that authorship and authenticity. Next we have the artwork itself. So owning an artwork isn’t the same as owning the copyright in the artwork.
And then there’s moral rights such as attribution and integrity which are separate rights. So in music and publishing, there is a common identifier such as the ISRC, the ISWC and in publishing we have ISBNs but in the visual art market, there is no widely adopted identifier connecting artworks, creators, to show the provenance and ownership across the market. So next we have exhibition. So owning an artwork, you can exhibit it, you can loan it out to a gallery and it’s the exhibition that really helps establish an artwork’s reputation, the provenance and the market value. So exhibition catalogs, photographs and condition reports document where and how the work was shown.
This helps authenticate the work yet often it remains disconnected from the artist’s other records. So next we have sales and transfers. There we go. So sales create further records. The global art market was estimated at 59.6 billion dollars in 2025 according to Art Basel and the UBS report.
So here we have invoices, we’ll have auction records, ownership documents and once again all these will establish who is the creator, establish the provenance, the chain of title, any resale royalty entitlements and so forth. And next we have collection management. So here we have artists, collectors and institutions that may have hundreds or even thousands of artworks to manage. So these records would cover ownership, condition, conservation, loans, insurance and estates. And then an artist can also license their work.
So meaning that in addition to selling it, they can license it for merchandising, for advertising, for different uses and so these also have records that need to be maintained. So now we look at the impact of AI on this entire life cycle. I first want to point out that AI is not new to art. It’s been around since the 1950s and in fact in the 70s, Harold Cohen’s Aaron has been around as well as contemporary artists of today like Rafik Anadol and they’ve long experimented with AI. But what’s different today is simply that it is not that AI can create the art, it’s how these models are trained and how independently they can generate those images.
So earlier AI artists really directed and had control of the code. There was a lot of human involvement whereas today’s models, you know, they generate sophisticated images from just simply prompts with sometimes little or none human intervention or control at all. So the issue is really the AI being trained on the work of others. And AI generated content is really putting a lot of pressure on the systems, how to define it, how to tell it apart from human-made work. Artists may use AI as a tool they direct.
