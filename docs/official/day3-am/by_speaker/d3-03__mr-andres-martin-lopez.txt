Most of us stream music every day without knowing what happens behind the scenes.
And it turns out what happens is really complex. So at the group we had to put effort and try to strip it down to the essentials while still staying true to how it really works. I’ll be covering the workflow and where AI meets it and then my colleague Lucas will talk about moral rights. As we go, please watch for two things. First, that the industry is built on an infrastructure that relies on identifiers, metadata and shared standards. You’re going to see where AI is putting pressure to that infrastructure and is also exposing gaps. In the diagram we try to capture that the fact that every song goes to the same basic pipelines.
It’s first created, then it’s rights and metadata get administered, it’s then licensed, distributed and finally we listen to it. You’re also going to see two lanes in the diagram and that’s because every track contains two copyright works. There’s the musical work or the composition and the sound recording or the master. Okay. Let’s start with creation.
Up at the top you have some writers, composers and lyricists that will create the musical work. This is the melodies, the notes, the lyrics. Then down below you have feature artists, producers and session musicians will turn it into a recording and it all comes together in the studio with the help of engineers and mixers. You will notice there’s a line connecting both lanes and that’s because every sound recording embodies an underlying composition. Next, the ownership is documented and generally this is done separately on each side.
For the musical work we have music publishers and collective management organizations or CMOs who will assign and capture the metadata, the identifiers and the rights. Then for the sound recording is the label, the distributor or the CMO who will do the same. In this domain we have performers that have performance rights which can be included with the recording rights and this depends on their agreements with labels or distributors. The master is the final artifact that will get licensed and that we will listen to and with it travels the identifiers and the metadata that will inform all the systems down the line. So now when it comes to licensing, we have two sets of parties holding or administering the rights.
We have the music publisher or CMO for the work and then we have the label, the distributor or the CMO for the sound recording. So anyone who wants to use music like a streaming service needs to get a license on both sides in exchange for payments. Then under the relevant licensing agreements, the master is then released to streaming services with all the metadata and identifiers and this information is really important because metadata and identifiers will let every system down the line identify what track has been played and will make royalty accounting and payment possible. Then once it reaches the streaming service, the metadata is then used to catalog the music and this is what will help people find it through search, recommendation or playlist. And because the data is tied to licensing, the money can flow back to creators.
But this is not a straight line. Musical work payments go to the publisher or CMO, then to the song writers and recording payments go to the label, the distributor or CMO and then to those involved in making the recording. This is the traditional industry workflow and again runs on shared infrastructure that relies on identifiers, metadata and standards. And now AI enters in the picture and it turns out that it touches all five stages. So for that reason we create a third lane underneath.
Starting with creation, AI is helping with lyrics and melodies and is showing up as AI instruments, AI vocals and AI mixing and mastering. Taking together all these pieces is what produces an AI assisted creation which already raises questions about licensing, ownership and infrastructure needed to actually support them. And this leads to a very practical question, how do you tell human generated music from AI generated music? And it turns out that answering this question is very important. Because whether music is protected by copyright and so whether there are rights to license and royalties to pay may depend on that answer.
At the group we look at different technologies that can help answering that question at this stage. From proven signals that can be captured in the digital audio workstation, metadata that can bind identity and authorship to the file, audio fingerprinting and zero technical knowledge proofs that can verify a claim without exposing the session. And again metadata is at the center at this stage. Let’s talk a little bit about AI models because creations go to them as prompts and then the outputs come back into the creative process. And models draw on training data that often includes copyright protected music which has implications for licensing status while at the same time creates possible new sources of revenue for the industry.
In this stage hold on. Yes, in this stage there is also attribution technology that has been developed that’s trying to connect an output to a specific material in the training set. But AI is also shaping downstream stages. Discovery algorithms already change the way we find music. There are AI detectors that are trying to tell whether a track is AI generated or not or what percentage of the track is AI generated.
There are monitoring and compliance tools that check if what was licensed is what actually is being used. And AI may also be deployed to help with some longstanding royalty administration challenges for streaming services or CMOs. Where does all this training data come from? Well, partly from a growing number of licensing arrangements. Depending on the model, developers may license the musical work, the recording or both. Music also comes in through opt-in schemes, creative common licenses, web scraping and sometimes original music is also being created for the purpose of training.
But music made with AI doesn’t stay in its own name. It actually flows back and reenters the system to the usual route. It goes to registration, administration, all the way to payments. And CARIS opens questions about how much of it is protected by copyright. So let’s step back.
Three things stood out for us at the Working Group. First, that the money still flows through the infrastructure the industry built held together by metadata and identifiers. Second is that AI brings new opportunities like possible new sources of revenue for rights holders and creators. But it’s also putting pressure to this infrastructure which will need to evolve. For example, it needs to be able to capture how much AI was involved in the making of a piece of music.
And we’ll have to know how to treat it according to the context. And finally, the gaps. Because for AI, the equivalent infrastructure isn’t in place yet. There’s no established way or common standard that can connect an AI generated output to the music in its training data and on to payment. This industry is very interconnected.
