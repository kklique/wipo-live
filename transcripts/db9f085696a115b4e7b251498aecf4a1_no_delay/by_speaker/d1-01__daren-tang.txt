# [d1-01] Opening Remarks
# 발표자: Daren Tang — Director General, World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO)  (speaker)
# Day 1 · 2026-11-09 · 예정 10:00-10:10 (Geneva) · 발언 1회 · 30문장 · 1,325단어 · 구분 방식: marker

Thank you. Ministers, Excellencies, Heads of IFU Officers, Distinguished Delegates, dear colleagues, dear friends, good morning and welcome to the first global forum on IP and AI.
Let me start by thanking the Ambassador of Kazakhstan, Master Karat Torabaev, for being our Chair over the next two days.
And while WIPO has dealt with IP and AI issues for the past few years, this is the first time that we are convening the global community to discuss critical issues across the whole spectrum of IP rights. patterns, trademarks, designs, copyrights and many, many others. Over the next two days, we will look widely across this fast-moving landscape, grapple with difficult issues, and put our heads together on a wide range of topics ranging from authorship and inventorship to AI training, human likeness and the impact of AI on the administration and governance of IP.
But this morning I want to start somewhere else.
I want to start with us as human beings. Humankind has been innovating for as long as we have existed. Fire, tools, agriculture, the wheel. cities and eventually modern science and technology all came from our ability to observe the world around us. imagine something different. and turn that imagination into reality. And we have been creating for just as long. Some of the oldest traces our ancestors left behind are not tools for survival, but cave paintings of incredible beauty that still haunt us today and carvings of bone stone and ivory, there were objects of beauty, instruments of music, and markers of ritual.
But in 1474, the way that we innovated itself underwent a revolutionary innovation with the birth of Intellectual Property. In that year, the Republic of Venice adopted what is rightly regarded as the world's first Patent Statute. Venice was then one of the great commercial and technological powers of the world. and its statute spoke explicitly about attracting people of unusual ingenuity and encouraging them to invent things. that would be useful to the Republic of Venice. It offered inventors protection for 10 years against others reproducing their inventions without permission.
In return, these inventions would be placed in a register would be open for public inspection. turbocharging the spread of technical knowledge. This was not done out of an extract love of the concept, for the concept of IP. but Venice existed in fierce competition with other cities and powers. Technology, knowledge and highly skilled people were sources of economic and strategic advantage. Venice, therefore, needed a way to encourage talented people to come, to invent, to disclose their inventions and ultimately to put those inventions to productive use.
And from that we can draw an insight that remains at the heart of the IP system today. Human ingenuity has tremendous value, but societies need institutions that enable that value to move from the mind of an inventor into community economy and society. IP stepped into this institutional role and became a transformative force in the history of human innovation.
Therefore, that innovative technological and strategic needs of society was what created the need for IP, and that is what remains at the heart of the IP system.
The distinction matters enormously at this point, at this moment in history, because if technology changes profoundly.
And if the way society creates, innovates and exchanges value, changes a bit, then we cannot assume that the institutions we have inherited, including the IP system, will always remain perfectly adapted to the world in which it serves. This is where artificial intelligence enters the picture. AI is often described as another technological revolution. but I am increasingly convinced that description does not quite capture what is happening.
Humanity has of course invented extraordinary general purpose technologies before. Machines extended our physical strength. The printing press reproduced and spread knowledge exponentially. Electricity transformed our ability to produce and transport energy. Computers expanded our ability to calculate and present information and Internet transformed communication distribution, trade, connection.
and commerce. But AI is doing something different. It is reaching into activities that we have historically associated with the human mind itself. reasoning analysis, synthesis. and for an increasing number of people, even cognition, creation and invention. For an organisation concerned with IP, that matters profoundly. because AI is not simply transforming another industry.
It is beginning to transform the processes from which Intellectual Property itself emerges – imagination, conception and expression. We really see AI helping scientists search chemical spaces that no human could explore manually. We have seen analysing medical images, designing new materials, writing sophisticated computer code, generating content and tackling increasingly complex mathematical problems.
singly providing solutions to some problems that eluded the best human minds for decades.
At the same time, a creator can now discover that his or her voice, face or style, has been reproduced synthetically at extraordinary fidelity.
Content of all types, music, films, videos and others, are being produced and distributed at rates which are overwhelming.
WIPO's description of this forum deliberately captures both sides of that reality.
AI is expanding the possibilities for invention and creativity while raising fundamental questions about rights, ownership and responsibility. And this development is moving extremely quickly. For some time now, commentators have tried to grapple with the deeper question of whether a machine truly thinks, understands, imagines or might one day even become conscious.
Those questions matter, of course. But given that AI has consistently broken every single prediction of the speed of development and the day is, in fact, coming where AI can itself create AI autonomously. we must already begin to address the practical implications of this technology and what AI will mean for humanity. Will it help us to find treatments for diseases that have resisted generations of medical research?
Will it accelerate solutions in food, energy and climate technologies, where it reviews new materials, new mathematics and perhaps even new insights into the fundamental laws of nature. I sincerely hope so. But we also have to confront other possibilities. What happens to work when machines can perform an ever-large share of cognitive and physical tasks? What happens to human creators and machines can reproduce their voices, styles and creative expressions at almost no marginal cost.
What happens to invention when AI becomes not simply an instrument used by an inventor, but an increasingly consequential participant the inventive process. And ultimately, how do we preserve human agency, dignity and meaning in a world in which some of the attributes we have considered uniquely human increasingly have analogous or even equivalent machine counterparts.
I confess that I first began preparing for today's speech, my instinct was to make this rather safe. balanced, moderate reassuring UN -style. But the more I thought about the scale and speed of what is happening, the less honest that seemed. because this may no longer be a moment for safe conversations for wait and see, for business as usual. The decisions that the technology companies, creators, innovators and institutions take over just the next few years will shape the relationship between humanity and technology for generations to come.
IP is, of course, only one part of that much larger story.
But it is an important part, and for those of us gathered under this roof, it is our part.
So I will propose three fundamental questions for us to confront as the global IP community.
First, what should the IP system protect and incentivize in the age of AI?
The modern IP system, the patent system of Venice and then the first corporate statutes may have been birthed during the Ménus Ones and the Age of Enlightenment, but came into maturity during the industrial age, the 1880s. It therefore reflects the economic architecture of that period. Then came computers and the digital revolution about 100 years later. Software became critical. Algorithms became enormously valuable.
Data became an essential economic resource.
The Internet transformed reproduction and distribution. The IP system adapted.
