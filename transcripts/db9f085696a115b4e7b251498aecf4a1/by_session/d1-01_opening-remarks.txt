But in 1474, the way that we innovated itself underwent a revolutionary innovation with the birth of Intellectual Property.
In that year, the Republic of Venice adopted what is rightly regarded as the World's First Patent Statute. Venice was then one of the great commercial and technological powers of the world, and its statute spoke explicitly about attracting people of unusual ingenuity and encouraging them to invent things that would be useful to the Republic of Venice. It offered inventors protection for 10 years against others reproducing their inventions without permission. In return, these inventions would be placed in a register that would be open for public inspection.

Technology, knowledge and highly skilled people were sources of economic and strategic advantage. Venice, therefore, needed a way to encourage talented people to come, to invent, to disclose their inventions and ultimately to put those inventions to productive use.
And from that we can draw an insight that remains at the heart of the IP system today. Human ingenuity has tremendous value, but societies need institutions that enable that value to move from the mind of an inventor into community, economy and society. IP stepped into this institutional role and became a transformative force in the history of human innovation.
Therefore, that innovative technological and strategic needs of society was what created the need for IP, and that is what remains at the heart of the IP system.
