In that year, the Republic of Venice adopted what is rightly regarded as the World's First Patent Statute. Venice was then one of the great commercial and technological powers of the world, and its statute spoke explicitly about attracting people of unusual ingenuity and encouraging them to invent things that would be useful to the Republic of Venice. Human ingenuity has tremendous value, but societies need institutions that enable that value to move from the mind of an inventor into community, economy and society. This is where artificial intelligence enters the picture. AI is often described as another technological revolution, but I am increasingly convinced that description does not quite capture what is happening. Humanity has, of course, invented extraordinary general purpose technologies before.
Machines extended our physical strength.
The printing press reproduced and spread knowledge exponentially.
Electricity transforms our ability to produce and transport energy. Computers expanded our ability to calculate and present information, and the Internet transformed communication, distribution, trade, connection, and commerce.
But AI is doing something different.
It is reaching into activities that we have historically associated with the human mind itself, reasoning, analysis, synthesis, and for an increasing number of people, even cognition, creation and invention.
For an organisation concerned with IP, that matters profoundly. Because AI is not simply transforming another industry, it is beginning to transform the processes from which Intellectual Property itself emerges – imagination, conception and expression.
We already see AI helping scientists search chemical spaces that no human could explore manually.
We have seen analyzing medical images, designing new materials, writing sophisticated computer code, generating content, and tackling increasingly complex mathematical problems, similarly providing solutions to some problems that have eluded the best human minds for decades.
At the same time, a creator can now discover that his or her voice, face, or style has been reproduced synthetically at extraordinary fidelity. Content of all types, music, films, videos and others, are being produced and distributed at rates which are overwhelming. WIPO's description of this Forum deliberately captures both sides of that reality. AI is expanding the possibilities for invention and creativity while raising fundamental questions about rights, ownership and responsibility. And this development is moving extremely quickly.
For some time now, commentators have tried to grapple with the deeper question of whether a machine truly thinks, understands, feels, imagines or might one day even become conscious. Those questions matter, of course. But given that AI has consistently broken every single prediction of its speed of development, and the day is in fact coming where AI can itself create AI autonomously, we must already begin to address the practical implications of this technology and what AI will mean for humanity.
Will it help us to find treatments for diseases that have resisted generations of medical research? where it accelerates solutions in food, energy and climate technologies, where it reviews new materials, new mathematics and perhaps even new insights into the fundamental laws of nature, I sincerely hope so. But we also have to confront other possibilities. What happens to work when machines can perform an ever-large share of cognitive and physical tasks.
What happens to human creators and machines can reproduce their voices, styles and creative expressions at almost no marginal cost.
What happens to invention when AI becomes not simply an instrument used by an inventor, but an increasingly consequential participant in the inventive process and ultimately how do we preserve human agency, dignity and meaning in a world in which some of the attributes we have considered uniquely human increasingly have analogous or even equivalent machine counterparts. I confess that I first began preparing for today's speech, my instinct was to make this rather safe. balanced, moderate, reassuring, UN style.

Because this may no longer be a moment for safe conversations, for wait and see, for business as usual. The decisions that governments, technology companies, creators, innovators and institutions take over just the next few years will shape the relationship between humanity and technology for generations to come. IP is of course only one part of that much larger story, but it is an important part, and for those of us gathered under this roof, it is our part.
So I will propose three fundamental questions for us to confront as a global IP community. First, what should the IP system protect and incentivize in the age of AI? The modern IP system, the patent system of and then the first corporate statutes may have been birthed during the Renaissance and Age of Enlightenment, but came into maturity during the industrial age, the 1880s.
It therefore reflects the economic architecture of that period.
Then came computers and the digital revolution about 100 years later. Software became critical. Algorithms became enormously valuable. Data became an essential economic resource. The Internet transformed reproduction and distribution. The IP system adapted.
Here at WIPO, for example, the WIPO Internet Treaties of the 1990s, which will celebrate their 30th anniversary in a few weeks' time, were a very important and rapid response to those digital transformations.
Much of this adaptation has involved adding layers onto an existing architecture whose basic categories were developed for an earlier industrial economy without having to rethink the architecture. AI is forcing us to turn our attention to the underlying architecture and to ask ourselves fundamental questions about its continued relevance. Who is the inventor when AI makes a material contribution to an invention? who is the author when human creativity and machine generation become increasingly intertwined. On what basis and under what conditions should copyright protected material be used for AI training?
What are the remuneration and compensation models?
How can we say we respect copyright and yet pay nothing for it?
How should we think about software, algorithms and data as sources of economic value?
And we are now confronting something even more fundamental.
What are the attributes of human identity itself?
Our voices, our images, our likenesses, our biometric characteristics, even our thoughts.
AI can replicate these in ways that earlier generations could barely have imagined. As Isaac Asimov said, or when technology is sufficiently advanced, it is indistinguishable from magic. Yet the legal protections surrounding them remain fragmented across IP, privacy, the system of IP registration and protection, which we must remember is the starting point for IP commercialization, has to operate at the speed of the innovators and creators and the economies and societies that it serves.
Much of today's IP regime and infrastructure was built around industrial innovation cycles measured in the lifespan of years. But digital innovation cycles operates much faster and AI will accelerate these cycles even further. Imagine an entrepreneur building a company whose AI-enabled technology fundamentally changes several times during the period in which an IP right is being examined.
Imagine IP offices confronted with vastly increased volumes of AI-assisted applications, by the way, it is already reality, or expanding bodies of machine-generated prior art.
Imagine creators trying to license millions of machine-mediated users through transaction models designed for another era.
The question is therefore not only whether we are able to protect what is valuable in the age of AI, but also whether the machinery of IP protection is fast enough, accessible enough and adaptable enough for the AI -enabled innovators and creators it now has to serve. If we do not get this right, we will find ourselves in a situation where innovators and creators will find that the IP system simply does not serve them any more.
They will not file, and And as some of them have told me, their IP strategy will increasingly be speed to market. So we need to have a frank conversation about what new processes, timelines and procedures need to be put in place for the IP system to remain relevant in a world where innovation and business cycles move not just in years but increasingly in months and weeks. Third, how do we make the economics work?
Perhaps this is the most practical question of all.
AI depends on enormous quantities of knowledge, culture, information and data. That value has been created by human beings and creators rightly one recognition and sustainable livelihoods. At the same time, innovators and AI companies need the ability to develop and train powerful models. Researchers need room to experiment. Businesses need predictable and workable rules. Consumers want access to better and more affordable products and services.
And society needs both continued human creativity and continued technological innovation.
These interests will sometimes conflict as they have in the past few years, but we should resist reducing this debate into a simple contest between creators and technology, or between those who want protection and those who want access.
IP has spent centuries dealing with precisely this part of problems, how to create incentives while enabling ideas to circulate, how to protect the current generation of creators while enabling the future generation of creators to emerge.
So the question cannot simply be who has the legal power to say no.
We must also ask how can we create workable ways for people to say yes.
